A troubled program or a doomed one? Five tells

By Kunjar Bhaduri · Published October 27, 2026 · Last reviewed October 27, 2026

I've led the delivery rescue of a troubled multi-year wealth-platform program, and I've taken a $12M loss-making technology division to $40M in revenue and profitability with zero layoffs. Neither one started healthy.

The question I'd want answered first in any program like that, before another dollar goes in, is whether it's troubled or doomed. Troubled means it can be saved with work. Doomed means the kindest thing is to stop it, and each month of delay costs more than the one before.

These are the five tells I look at. The first decides whether to go on at all. The other four decide where to start.

1. Does the reason still hold?

Ask the sponsor to say, in one sentence, what the business gets when this is done. Then ask whether that is still true.

Programs can outlive their reasons. A market moves, a business is reorganized, a deadline changes, and the build keeps going on momentum because stopping means someone has to say the money is spent. If the reason is gone, the other four tells don't matter. Stop it, keep what can be reused, and say so plainly.

2. Does someone own the shared parts?

Every large program has a few parts everything else depends on. In a wealth platform those are client onboarding and the data model for families, members, entities and accounts. If nobody owns them, each team builds its own version, and defects pile up where the versions meet.

This one is fixable. On the rescue I took ownership of client onboarding and the data model, and within six months defect leakage was down 30% and feature delivery was up 40%.

3. Do defects cluster?

Pull the last few months of production defects and sort them by where they came from. If most come from a handful of places, you have your work list. If they come from everywhere, the design is the problem, and patching faster won't save it.

4. Can something small ship soon?

Ask the team to name one small, real thing a user will touch in the next few weeks. Not a demo and not a milestone on a slide. Something in production.

If they can name it, the program can still finish things, and finishing small things is how trust comes back. If nobody can, the program has forgotten how to finish, and that takes longer to fix than any technical problem.

5. Does the client still argue?

Watch the client in the next steering meeting. A client who is angry and pushing for dates still wants the thing. A client who has gone quiet, sends a deputy and stops asking questions has started planning for life without it.

Anger you can work with. Quiet is the worse sign.

The scorecard

  1. The reason still holds. Troubled: the sponsor states it in one sentence and it is still true. Doomed: nobody can state it, or it stopped being true.
  2. Someone owns the shared parts. Troubled: nobody owns them yet, but you can name who should. Doomed: several teams each defend their own version.
  3. Defects cluster. Troubled: a handful of sources. Doomed: everywhere.
  4. Something small can ship soon. Troubled: the team names it. Doomed: nobody can.
  5. The client still argues. Troubled: angry and pushing for dates. Doomed: quiet, sending a deputy.

What I would do on Monday

  1. Run the first tell with the sponsor in one sentence. If it lands on doomed, stop and salvage.
  2. Name the owner of the shared parts and the defect clusters, and put both on the board.
  3. Pick one small production thing a user will touch in the next few weeks and protect it.
